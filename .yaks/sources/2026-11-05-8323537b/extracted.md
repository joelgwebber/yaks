It should come as a surprise to no one at Proton, least of all in engineering, that software engineering has irrevocably changed this year if not before. LLMs and their associated tooling have gained the ability to write code to the point where a senior Proton engineer told me a few days ago, and I quote, "No one writes code in {business unit name} anymore." Like memorizing phone numbers in the 20th century or riding horses in the 19th, writing great code manually is a core skill whose time has passed.

That is in general a fantastic thing. Much of code writing was and is time-consuming drudgery, at least in production and at scale. Documentation and testing are both necessary things that few like doing and historically have often been neglected at the cost of poor communication, inefficiencies and reliability. Boilerplate is the epitome of software engineering grind, and it may never be something a human being has to worry about again. Models can now advise and propose architectures and system designs that, while often imperfect, contribute meaningfully to discussion, decision-making, and education.

Outside Proton, the AI revolution is taking the world by storm. A lot of that is fluff and superficial, cosmetic campaigns more to be seen as innovative rather than truly be innovative. But much of it is very real, and the investments those companies (including our competitors) are making now are going to determine the business environment we operate in the future. This is not about chasing trends—we are being left behind here, and it is an **existential threat to the success of the mission itself**.

So why, at Proton, does it not *feel* like the revolution has come? Why has productivity not increased dramatically if everyone is using LLMs all the time already? I believe it comes down to three things, all of which we will need to correct if we are to be successful in the new era that is **already here today**, not on its way.

The first is a change in engineering culture and engineer mentality. **Your job has fundamentally changed. It is no longer to write code, and LLMs are not here to help you write better code faster. Your job is to help the LLM write better code faster**. Every engineer is now a team leader, a manager, and your direct reports are the models and agents you use. Guiding them directly and correcting them when they are wrong are part of it, for sure. But a team of two people who always pair-programmed together would not be much of a team, nor would a team where no one worked when the team leader was in a meeting. AI needs to be able to work autonomously and continuously with less and less human babysitting. Your job is to identify problems that the LLM cannot solve on its own and help them not only solve them this time but be able to better solve them next time, not just for you but for everyone: repository-level context files, shared skills and prompts, and habit of ensuring that repeated failures trigger an investigation and a fix. Your job is to push the limits of what the LLM can do autonomously, step in for the things that it cannot, and always think of how you can improve the status quo.
